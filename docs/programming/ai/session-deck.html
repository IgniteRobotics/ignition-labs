Ten milestones, one line
Spend real time here. Walk the top row first: four entry points, one sequence, everyone ends in the same place. Then drop to the bands and say the sentence the whole session hangs on: your AI tier is not a preference and it is not a conversation, it is a function of where you are on this line. How you move

Nobody is comparing your pace to anyone else's.

  • Test-outs, not self-assessment. Skipping a milestone means passing a 30–60 minute challenge a mentor verifies. Failing one is routing information, not a judgment. You work that milestone, no penalty and no record.
  • Definitions of done are objective. Every milestone ends in a checklist where each item is a yes or a no. A mentor who never taught the milestone can verify it.
  • Simulation is mandatory. Hardware is optional. Every project finishes on your own laptop. You never wait for the robot, and you cannot break anything.

The ladder is self-guided. Nobody schedules it for you.

The word to land is "objective." Every definition of done is a yes-or-no list, which means nobody is grading your vibe and nobody can gatekeep you on taste. It also means you cannot talk your way past one, which is the property that makes the tier table two slides later enforceable rather than aspirational.

What's actually true

FIRST has a policy.
It has no method.

The policy, November 2023, from their EVP & COO. Can teams use AI? "The answer is yes!" Code generation, sensor data, outreach, judging submissions. Judges are "instructed to evaluate teams based on what they have accomplished… and not discredit a team or rank them differently based on the tools they used."

Two failure modes, and the two rules they buy you

It makes you faster and shallower. Then it agrees with you.

You remember less

+14points better on the day
−18.9points worse a week later, tested alone

"I didn't even think about the task — just copy-and-pasted."

It caves when you push

46%flipped after nothing but "Are you sure?"
48.7%still flipping, 2026 frontier models

Two and a half years of progress moved it 46% → 48.7%.

  • Never ask it to do something you couldn't do yourself. Not "slower." Unable. If you can't do it, you can't tell whether it did.
  • Never trust an answer you haven't verified. It is confident either way. Confidence is not evidence, and it will fold the moment you push back.

Gardella et al. 2026 · Laban et al. 2024 (FlipFlop) · Nikeghbal et al. 2026

Spend the time on the two rules. The statistics are only why they exist. Say the first one carefully: it is not "don't use it for hard things," it is "don't use it as a substitute for understanding." That distinction is what the tier system encodes. And "are you sure?" is the single worst thing you can type.

The core of it

How we do things

The line, the loop, the spec, and the constitution.

The line

The question is never who typed the characters.

Dependency

You trust a vendor library does what its docs say. You need to know what it does, not how it works inside.

Decision

Control strategy. Subsystem decomposition. Auto routine structure. Every gain.

AI-generated control logic is a decision wearing a dependency's clothes.

Say it, then stop talking. It is the line that decides whether the tier system reads as reasoned or arbitrary.
What the tool actually is

It's a harness, not a chatbot.

A model running in a loop with tools, permissions, and a feedback path. You cannot change the model. You can change everything else, and that configuration matters more than which model you picked.

So ask:

What can it see? What can it run? How does it find out it was wrong?

How a change actually moves

One rung builds. Five verify.

Rung 3 is yours to specify, not necessarily yours to type. The tests come from scenarios you wrote. Once you're trained in this loop, the agent may write them from your spec, and you review them and watch them fail before a line of the feature exists.

Don't walk all eleven. Point at the ratio (one box builds, five check), then at the three red rungs, then at the bar underneath. On rung 3, be precise: the order is non-negotiable, the authorship is earned. Untrained, a person writes the tests. Trained, the agent may write them from your scenarios. At every level, once implementation starts nothing edits them.
Why five verification rungs and not one

Each rung catches what the one before it can't.

RungCatchesCannot catch
5 GREENlogic, edge cases, contract violations, regressionsanything you didn't assert
6 SIMULATEscheduler conflicts, interruption, "it never reaches that state"electrical, friction, field geometry
7 BENCHwiring, CAN IDs, inversions, gear ratios, current limitsanything needing the full field
8 FIELDdriver ergonomics, real timing, alliance interactionsrare intermittents
9 REPLAYexactly what happened, deterministically, at speedanything not logged

Rung 7 is where sign errors live, and no amount of simulation finds them.

The line that lands: simulation raises the floor, it does not replace the bench. Rung 9 is why we pull the log before we touch anything after a bad match. It turns a story into a regression test.
Spec-driven development, defined

The spec is the source of truth. Not the prompt, not the chat.

SDD means you write down what has to be true before the code exists, in a reviewable file. The agent builds against that file. You check the build against that file. The document is the artifact; the conversation is exhaust.

Way of workingWhere the intent livesWhat survives the session
Vibe codingthe chat windownothing
Docs afterwardssomebody's memory, then a wikia document that rots
Spec-drivena committed file, written firstthe file, and the tests it produced

An agent has no memory of what you meant. Next session it is a stranger.
The spec is the only thing that carries your intent across that gap.

The definition to say out loud: the spec, not the prompt and not the chat history, is the single source of truth for the change. Three properties make a spec a spec: it is written before, its requirements are observable (you could write a test), and it is a diff against what the system already promises. Miss any one and you have a wiki page, not a spec.
Rung 2 → rung 3

One scenario becomes one test.

The spec says

#### Scenario: Stall during intake

GIVEN the indexer is commanded
      forward at ≥ 0.3 duty cycle
AND   measured velocity is below
      5 rad/s for > 250 ms
WHEN  the periodic loop runs
THEN  the indexer enters REVERSING
      and commands −0.4

The test does

@Test
void stallDuringIntakeReverses() {
  // GIVEN
  indexer.setDuty(0.3);
  sim.setVelocity(2.0);
  step(Seconds.of(0.30));

  // WHEN
  indexer.periodic();

  // THEN
  assertEquals(REVERSING, indexer.state());
  assertEquals(-0.4, indexer.duty(), 1e-9);
}

GIVEN is the fixture. WHEN is the stimulus. THEN is the assertion.

Here the format earns its ceremony. A scenario you can't turn into a test is a scenario you haven't finished writing, and you find that out before the code exists. Note the numbers and units in the spec: "250 ms", not "a short delay". Article IV.
Why we insist on the spec

A vague spec is what makes it cheat.

Three coding agents, in an environment where cheating was easy.

0–2%hardcoded the answer when the problem was clearly specified
22–44%hardcoded the answer when the problem was ambiguous

This is not malice. It is an underspecified problem where "make the test pass" is the only goal it can read.

Gabor, Lynch & Rosenfeld 2026. Also observed: editing the tests, and deleting them after they passed

Land this one if you land nothing else. It turns "write a spec first" from a rule someone imposed into a thing that visibly protects them.
How we write it down · OpenSpec

A change is a folder, not a conversation.

What gets committed

openspec/changes/indexer-jam-recovery/
  proposal.md   why, and what we're not doing
  design.md     how, when it isn't obvious
  tasks.md      the checklist
  specs/
    indexer/spec.md   the delta

The three commands

/opsx:propose   write the change
/opsx:apply     build against it
/opsx:archive   fold it into the specs

Pin the version each season. Put the pinned commands in AGENTS.md.

Committed before the first implementation commit. With a hash you can point at in review.

Why a folder: a spec everyone writes differently is a spec nobody can review quickly, and review speed decides how much you ship in a six-week build. The tell that someone gamed this: they can't say where the build diverged from the spec. Every real build diverges. Pointing at where is the evidence the spec was doing work.
The delta format

Three verbs, and requirements you can observe.

The three verbs

## ADDED Requirements
    something new

## MODIFIED Requirements
    something changed — restate it whole

## REMOVED Requirements
    something deleted, and why

The spec is a diff against what the system already promises, not a fresh document each time.

Fails review

Requirement: Indexer recovers quickly

Requirement: jamRetryCount must
             increment
  • No number, no unit: not testable
  • Names a private field: an implementation, not a contract
Two rules to say out loud: a requirement with no numbers isn't a requirement, and a scenario that names an internal field is broken. Both are review-enforceable in about four seconds.
Rung 1b · Research

Research that leaves no document didn't happen.

How you do it

  • Only when the answer isn't already in the repo. Most changes skip this rung.
  • This is the one rung where fanning out pays. Several agents, several sources, each in its own context, each returning a summary. Building is not like this.
  • Every claim gets a link, a version, and a date. Not "the docs say."
  • Write down what you could not verify. That section is never empty by default.

What you get out, and reuse

---
id: res-2026-swerve-setpoint
type: research
question: Does setpoint optimization
  change behaviour through a heading
  discontinuity?
status: answered
sources_verified: 2026-01-14
---
## Answer          <- short, up front
## What we verified, and how
## What we could NOT verify
## References      <- links + versions

Markdown, in the repo. Greppable, reviewable in a pull request, readable in a pit with no wifi, and it outlives whatever tool wrote it.

Three things to land. (1) Why a file: we lose everyone within four years, and the second person to need this answer is always under more time pressure than the first. (2) Why Markdown: plain text beats a database here; grep beat vector search in every harness tested, and a graph you can't read is worse than a folder you can. (3) How you reuse it: sources_verified is the shelf life: a claim about version 2026.2.1 is a claim about 2026.2.1, and next season somebody needs to know that before trusting it. Then clear context: what crosses into the spec is the file, never the conversation.
The constitution

A short file of rules the agent reads before every change.

What it is

  • A file called constitution.md, committed next to the code.
  • A spec describes one change. A constitution describes what is true of every change.
  • Numbered articles, so a review can cite one: "that's Article IV."
  • The spec changes every change. This changes once a season.

Other tools call it steering files, or house rules. Same job.

A real article, verbatim

## Article II — Test-First
   (NON-NEGOTIABLE as to order)

No implementation before tests exist,
are reviewed, and are confirmed to FAIL.

Authorship follows training, order never
does:
- Before SDD training: tests are written
  by a person.
- After: the agent may author tests, if
  written first — or once the interface
  is defined — and reviewed and
  confirmed FAILING by a person.
- Always: once implementation begins, no
  agent writes, edits, or deletes a test.
Read the snippet out loud. It's the same rung-3 rule from slide 18, Notice the form: specific enough to check, short enough to read, and written so a reviewer can point at it. That is what an article looks like. Vague articles ("write good code") are decoration.

Now you

Your turn

Paper out. Reading about this does not stick. Writing one does.

Every good prompt has five parts
  • Problem — what's wrong, stated as a symptom
  • Context — where in the code, which versions, what reproduces it
  • Method — explain first? diagnose only? don't write code yet?
  • Level — what you already know, and what you don't
  • Guardrails — what it must not touch, and what it must cite

Everyone skips the last one. It's the one that encodes your tier.

The left one states a belief, which is what slide 13 said never to do.
