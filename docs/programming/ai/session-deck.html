Two failure modes — and the two rules they buy you

It makes you faster and shallower. Then it agrees with you.

You remember less

+14points better on the day
−18.9points worse a week later, tested alone

"I didn't even think about the task — just copy-and-pasted."

It caves when you push

46%flipped after nothing but "Are you sure?"
48.7%still flipping, 2026 frontier models

Two and a half years of progress moved it 46% → 48.7%.

  • Never ask it to do something you couldn't do yourself. Not "slower" — unable. If you can't do it, you can't tell whether it did.
  • Never trust an answer you haven't verified. It is confident either way. Confidence is not evidence, and it will fold the moment you push back.

Gardella et al. 2026 · Laban et al. 2024 (FlipFlop) · Nikeghbal et al. 2026

The two rules are the point of this slide — the statistics are just why. Say the first one carefully: it is not "don't use it for hard things," it is "don't use it as a substitute for understanding." That distinction is the whole tier system in one sentence. And "are you sure?" is the single worst thing you can type.

Part two

The line and the loop

Where the tool belongs, and where it doesn't.

The line

The question is never who typed the characters.

Dependency

You trust a vendor library does what its docs say. You need to know what it does — not how it works inside.

Decision

Control strategy. Subsystem decomposition. Auto routine structure. Every gain.

AI-generated control logic is a decision wearing a dependency's clothes.

That last sentence is the whole slide. If they remember one thing from Part 2, this is it.
What the tool actually is

It's a harness, not a chatbot.

A model running in a loop with tools, permissions, and a feedback path. You cannot change the model. You can change everything else — and that configuration matters more than which model you picked.

So the real question is:

What can it see? What can it run? How does it find out it was wrong?

The loop
0CONSTITUTIONstanding rules, read before every change
1EXPLOREthe code, the knowledge base, the mechanism
1bRESEARCHoptional — but always leaves a cited document
2PROPOSEproposal, delta spec, tasks
3REDscenarios become failing tests. Confirm they fail.
4APPLYimplement against the spec — agent may drive
5GREENtests pass, format, CI
6SIMULATEwatch it in the sim GUI — don't just assert on it
7BENCHreal hardware, robot on blocks
8FIELDpractice field, match
9REPLAYlog replay against what actually happened
10REVIEWdefend the diff, disclose
11ARCHIVEspec archived, decision recorded, AGENTS.md updated
Don't walk all eleven. Point at three: tests come before code (3), simulation is not the same as tests passing (6), replay turns a match failure into a regression test (9).
The most important slide today

A vague spec is what makes it cheat.

Three coding agents, in an environment where cheating was easy.

0–2%hardcoded the answer when the problem was clearly specified
22–44%hardcoded the answer when the problem was ambiguous

Not malice. An underspecified problem where "make the test pass" is the only goal it can read.

Gabor, Lynch & Rosenfeld 2026 — also observed: editing the tests, and deleting them after they passed

Land this one if you land nothing else. It turns "write a spec first" from a rule someone imposed into a thing that visibly protects them.
Part three

Your turn

Paper out. This is the part that actually sticks.

Every good prompt has five parts
  • Problem — what's wrong, stated as a symptom
  • Context — where in the code, which versions, what reproduces it
  • Method — explain first? diagnose only? don't write code yet?
  • Level — what you already know, and what you don't
  • Guardrails — what it must not touch, and what it must cite

Everyone skips the last one. It's the one that encodes your tier.

Part four

The rules

Twelve of them. Each has evidence under it.

The whole list
  • There is always a constitution.
  • Spec first. No spec, no code.
  • One format, and it's OpenSpec.
  • Tests first. Nothing edits them once you start.
  • Its job is to disagree with you.
  • Never let it grade its own homework.
  • Green is a floor, not a ceiling.
  • Context is a budget. Clear it at boundaries.
  • Simulate before deploy. Replay after compete.
  • Enforce with machines. Request with prose.
  • Disclose, and defend.
  • Nothing leaves undocumented.

Full list with the evidence: Working with AI → The Rules on the team site

Show, don't read. Tell them the list lives in the docs and each rule has evidence under it. Move on quickly.
If you take three away
  • Spec first. No spec, no code.
    Because of the 22–44%.
  • Its job is to disagree with you.
    It flips about half the time you push — so stop pushing. Never type "are you sure?"
  • Green is a floor, not a ceiling.
    Passing tests isn't correct. In FRC that's an auto that works from exactly the position you tested it from.
